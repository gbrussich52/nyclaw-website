Before you start

In our experience, many small-business AI projects stall within a month, and rarely because the tool was weak. The owner bought a chat tab, never named the one job it was meant to do, and had no way to tell in week four whether it was still doing it.

This guide covers the part that gets skipped. Pick one leak you can feel in dollars. Give the assistant a written brief of how your business works. Decide what it may do alone and what it must ask about. Then check the result for fifteen minutes every Monday.

It is short on purpose. You can read it in one sitting and start on Monday. It names no winning tool, and it contains no results we cannot show you the record for.

Part 1

Name one dollar leak

One job, one number, one owner. Everything else waits.

The businesses that keep an AI setup running did not start with "give me an AI employee." They started with one leak the owner could feel in dollars:

  • calls that go to voicemail and never get a reply;
  • web leads that sit in the inbox overnight;
  • quotes that go out and never get a follow-up;
  • appointments that nobody confirms, so people don't show.

Pick the one that costs you most. If you can't decide, pick the one you complain about most often. That is usually the same one.

Write three things down

Template · The leak
The job: [e.g. reply to every missed call with a text]
The number that shows it is done: [e.g. missed calls this week, and how many got a text the same hour]
Where that number lives today: [phone system report, CRM, a spreadsheet, or "nowhere yet"]
Today's number, before any AI: [count it for one week, or write an honest guess and mark it as a guess]
Who owns it: [a person's name, not "the AI" and not "the team"]

If the number lives nowhere, your first job is to start counting. A week of tally marks beats a month of guessing. Without a before-number you will never know whether the setup helped.

Keep the scope small. Automate the boring, common path. Leave odd cases to a person. A setup that handles the usual case well and hands the rest to you is worth more than one that tries everything and fails quietly.

Part 2

Write the one-page business brief

An assistant that doesn't know how your business works will guess. Write it down once.

Most people re-explain their business in every chat, then wonder why the answers sound generic. A one-page brief fixes that. Write it the way you would brief a sharp new hire on day one. Plain English. No more than a page.

Template · One-page business brief
Who we serve: [the customers you want, where they are, what they usually need]
What good looks like: [e.g. every lead gets a reply within the hour during business hours; every quote gets one follow-up]
Decisions already made: [prices we don't discount, services we don't offer, areas we don't serve, words we never use. These are not up for debate.]
Where the numbers live: [which system holds leads, quotes, bookings, invoices, and who can see each]
How we sound: [e.g. warm, short, no slang, sign off with first name. Paste two messages you were proud of.]
What it may do alone, and what it must ask about: [fill this in from Part 3]
Last updated: [date] by [name]

Where to put it

Most assistants now have a place for standing instructions, so you don't paste the brief every time. In Claude, a project holds its own instructions and files. In Gemini, a Gem is a custom version with written instructions. In others, look for custom instructions or a saved setup. See the tools page for sources.

Claude and ChatGPT can also remember things between chats. That helps, but memory is something the assistant picks up on its own. Your brief is something you control. Keep the brief, and every few weeks look at what the assistant has saved about you. Both let you review or turn memory off.

Keep secrets out

Never put passwords, card numbers or customer records in the brief. Say where they live and who may open them. Nothing more.

Part 3

Decide what it may do alone, and what it must ask about

Ask about too much and it is useless. Ask about too little and it does things you didn't mean.

Write two short lists and put them in the brief. Clear lists beat case-by-case judgment.

May do aloneMust ask first
Fix something that was already agreed and brokeSend anything to a new person for the first time
Re-send a confirmation that failedSpend money, or go past the monthly cost you set
Draft replies for a person to approveChange prices, offer discounts, promise dates
Sort, tag and summarize incoming messagesPost anything in public under your name
Update its own notes and recordsDelete customer data, or change who can log in
Flag a problem to the Monday ownerAnything new that nobody has discussed

Repair, or a new decision?

One question sorts most cases. Is this a repair, or a new decision? A repair restores something you already approved: the confirmation text that failed, the form that stopped sending, the follow-up that went out late. Repairs need no permission. A new decision creates something you have not approved: a first message to a stranger, a discount, a public post. New decisions always wait for a person.

Logins and access

Newer assistants can run in the background and hold their own logins. OpenAI's Dots, announced September 29, 2026, can be given "specific identities, credentials, and tools," per TechCrunch's report. The report describes no approval step. So the act/ask list is yours to enforce. Give each assistant the narrowest access that does the job. Use a separate account where you can. Write down how to turn its access off, and test that you can.

Ask for a useful answer, not a polite one

Example · Compliant vs useful

You ask: "Review our quote follow-up email."

A compliant answer: "This looks great! Here are a few things you might consider..." followed by five generic tweaks.

A useful answer: "The subject line is vague. The third paragraph repeats the second; cut it. The question you want answered is buried at the end; put it first. Here is the rewrite."

Put a line in your brief: "Tell me what is weak first. Give one recommendation, not a list of options." Use the same test on any vendor. If every answer is "great question," you are paying for agreement.

Part 4

Check it every Monday

Week one is not the hard part. Week four, when nobody is looking, is.

Most setups that fail "worked" in the demo. Then a form broke, a login expired, a scheduled task paused, or a person stopped reading the summary. Nothing announced it. Fifteen minutes every Monday catches it.

1

Look at the number

Open the number from Part 1. Up, flat or down from last week? Write it in the same place every week.

2

Test it yourself

Send a fake lead through the real path and confirm it arrives where it should. "No errors" is not proof. A message in the inbox is.

3

Fix one thing

If the number slipped, change one thing: the wording, the timing, who gets the alert. Don't rebuild everything.

4

Write one line

What broke, what you changed, what you expect next week. A shared note is enough.

The fake-lead test

Once a month, and after any website or phone change, test every way a customer can reach you:

  • Every form on the website, on a phone and on a computer
  • The main phone number, during hours and after hours
  • Any text or chat line, and any booking link
  • The email address on your site and your map listing

For each one, confirm the message reached a person or system that will act on it. Forms that show "Thank you" and send nothing are a common find. The visitor thinks you have their request. You never see it.

Ask how they found you

Add one field to your intake form and your call notes: "How did you hear about us?" Include choices for ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Meta AI and Perplexity next to Google, referral and social. More people now ask an assistant for a recommendation. A simple field tells you whether that is reaching you. No dashboard needed.

Stop rules

Decide in advance when the setup gets paused. Write these down with the name of the person who can pull the switch, and how.

  • A wrong or rude message reaches a customer
  • A customer says they don't want to be contacted, and the setup contacts them again
  • The number slips two weeks in a row and one fix didn't help
  • Nobody did the Monday check for two weeks
  • Monthly cost goes past the limit you set

Scheduled tasks raise the stakes. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok can now run jobs on a schedule. A job that runs unseen can fail unseen. ChatGPT, for one, may pause an unattended task after a period of inactivity, per 9to5Mac's June 2026 report. The Monday check is how you find out.

Part 5

How to tell an operator setup from a chatbot install

Whether you build it yourself or hire someone, this is what to look for.

The model is the engine. The harness is the car.

The model (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Meta AI) is the engine. It reads and writes. The harness is the car around it: the tools it may use (email, calendar, CRM, files), the brief it follows, the limits on what it may do and spend, and how someone checks its work. A better engine in no car still goes nowhere. Install the car, not just the engine.

Weak startOperator start
"We need AI" or "we need a chatbot""We lose leads after hours"
Buy tools firstName the leak and the number, then pick tools
Train staff on automation theoryShip one working path the owner can see
Declare victory at go-liveCheck the number every week for 90 days

Signs of an operator setup

  • There is a named first job and a number before anyone picks a tool.
  • Go-live includes a handoff: how to turn it off, who owns the Monday check, where the records are.
  • The builder talks about how it fails (forms that don't send, tasks that stop, costs that run away), not only demos.
  • After launch, a named person owns the check. If that person is "nobody," it will decay.

If a vendor only sells "we install ChatGPT for your team," ask what happens on day 45, when nobody has looked at the outputs. If the answer is silence, you bought a tab, not a working system.

Template · Questions to ask before you hire anyone
  1. What is the first job, and what number will show it works?
  2. Who owns the weekly check after you leave? What exactly do they look at?
  3. What do you add beyond the scheduled tasks, projects and memory already in the assistant we pay for?
  4. Which of our accounts and logins will it hold? How do we turn its access off?
  5. What happens when it fails? Who finds out, and how fast?
  6. What does it cost each month to run, and is there a cap?
  7. Who owns the setup and the brief if we stop working together?
  8. What would make you tell us not to automate this?

A good builder will also ask you: walk me through what happens from a new lead to a paid invoice; what have you tried before and what happened; who here will own this. If they ask none of that, be careful.

Three first jobs, sketched

Each sketch is a starting shape, not a promise. Your number tells you whether it works.

Sketch 1

Missed-call text-back

What it does: when a call goes unanswered, send a short text: who you are, that you saw the call, and a way to book or reply. Replies go to a person.

Ask first: anything beyond the first text; any quote or price.

Number to watch: missed calls each week, and how many got a text within the window you set.

Monday owner: whoever runs the front desk or the phones.

Sketch 2

Quote follow-up at 48 hours

What it does: when a quote has had no reply for 48 hours, draft a short, friendly follow-up in your voice. For the first month, a person approves each one before it sends.

Stops when: the customer replies, accepts, declines or asks you to stop; after one follow-up unless you set more.

Number to watch: quotes sent, how many got a follow-up on time, and how many got any reply.

Monday owner: the person who writes the quotes.

Sketch 3

Appointment confirmation, with stop rules

What it does: confirm each booking at the time you choose before the visit, with an easy way to reschedule.

Stops when: the customer confirms, cancels, reschedules or replies STOP; never sends at night; never sends to someone who has not agreed to texts. Check the texting consent rules that apply to you with your phone provider or lawyer.

Number to watch: bookings, confirmations, and no-shows each week.

Monday owner: whoever manages the schedule.

Part 6

Mistakes that kill setups

Three patterns we see again and again. Each has a plain fix.

1. Building the system instead of using it

The task board with twelve fields that took an afternoon to set up and was dead in two weeks. The dashboard nobody opens. It looks like progress and produces nothing.

The fix: keep the process small enough to explain in two sentences. A plain list with three headings (Doing, Next, Someday), updated daily and read weekly, outlasts the fancy version. If you haven't used a system in two weeks, it is dead. Delete it.

2. Going live without testing

Something gets built, looks fine, and goes live. Nobody sends a real test. The form shows "Thank you" and the message goes nowhere. The text-back sends to the wrong number. Nobody notices for weeks.

The fix: nothing is done until a test message has arrived at the real destination. Test again after every change. "It ran with no errors" is not the same as "it worked."

3. Chasing the newest tool

Every week brings a new model or app that tops some chart. Switching feels like diligence. It is usually distraction. Each switch means new setup, new logins, new things to break, and a brief to rewrite.

The fix: change tools when a specific job fails on the current one, not when a new one launches. Try a new tool for 30 days against your number before you commit. "It's interesting" is not a reason. "It fixes the job our current tool keeps getting wrong" is.

Checklist

Your first 30 days, and days 31 to 90

One page. Print it and tick it off.

Week 1 · Pick and measure

  • Name the leak, the number, and the owner (Part 1)
  • Count the number for one week, before any AI
  • Write the one-page brief (Part 2)

Week 2 · Set limits and build small

  • Write the act/ask lists and put them in the brief (Part 3)
  • Check what your current assistant already does before you buy anything
  • Build the smallest version that handles the common case
  • Send a fake lead through it and confirm it arrived

Week 3 · Go live on the boring path

  • Turn it on for the common case only; odd cases go to a person
  • Watch the first few runs yourself
  • Run the fake-lead test on every form and phone path
  • Add "How did you hear about us?" with AI assistant choices
  • Write the stop rules and who can pull the switch

Week 4 · First Monday checks

  • Do the 15-minute check (Part 4) and write one line
  • Fix one thing, or pause it
  • Update the brief with anything you learned

Days 31 to 90 · Keep it honest

  • Every Monday: number, fake-lead test, one fix, one line
  • Day 45: is anyone still reading the output? Is the number better than your before-count? Has any stop rule fired?
  • Day 90: decide. Keep it, change one thing and run another 90 days, or stop it. Write down why.
  • Only after a keep: pick the second leak
