The AI Operator's Playbook

How a small firm gets one job done by an AI assistant, and keeps it done

Facts checked November 15, 2026

By Giani Brussich, founder of NYClaw.io · hello@nyclaw.io

Download this guide as a PDF

Start here

This guide is for an owner or office manager who wants an AI assistant to take one repeating job off the firm's plate. It takes about fifteen minutes to read. You need no technical background.

The method has four steps. Name one leak that costs you money. Write a one-page brief of how your firm works. Decide what the assistant may do alone and what it must ask about. Check the result for fifteen minutes every Monday.

In this guide, the operator is the person who owns that one job: who sets it up, checks it each week, and can turn it off. That is you, or someone you name.

What this guide rests on. We run AI jobs in our own businesses, and we read the vendor pages listed under Sources. The guide reports no client results. Where it gives a number, the number is a suggestion for you to set.

Part 1

Name one leak

One job, one number, one owner. Everything else waits.

Start with a leak you can count. Some common ones:

  • Trades and front desks: missed calls with no reply, quotes with no follow-up, appointments nobody confirms.
  • Law offices: new inquiries that arrive after hours and wait until someone gets to them.
  • Staffing and recruiting: candidates who apply and hear nothing, and clients who wait on a status update.
  • Real estate: new listing inquiries that go unanswered, and showings nobody confirms.

Pick the one that costs you most. If you can't decide, pick the one you complain about most often.

Write five things down

The job: for example, reply to every missed call with a text
The number that shows it is done: for example, missed calls this week, and how many got a text within the hour
Where that number lives today: phone report, customer software, a spreadsheet, or "nowhere yet"
Today's number, before any AI: count it for one week
Who owns it: one person's name

If the number lives nowhere, your first job is to count. Keep a tally for one week. Without a starting number you cannot tell later whether the setup helped.

Keep the job small. Automate the common case and send the odd cases to a person.

Part 2

Write the one-page brief

An assistant that doesn't know how your firm works will guess.

Write the brief the way you would brief a sharp new hire on day one. Use plain words and keep it to one page.

Who we serve: the clients you want, where they are, what they usually need
What good looks like: for example, every inquiry gets a reply within the hour during business hours
Decisions already made: prices you don't discount, work you don't take, areas you don't serve, words you never use
Where the numbers live: which system holds leads, quotes, bookings and invoices, and who can see each
How we sound: for example, warm, short, no slang. Paste two messages you were proud of.
What it may do alone, and what it must ask about: from Part 3
How to answer me: "Tell me what is weak first. Give one recommendation."
Last updated: date and name

Where to put it

Most assistants have a place for standing instructions, so you don't paste the brief into every chat. In Claude and ChatGPT it is a project. In Gemini it is a skill. Appendix A lists the vendor pages.

Claude and ChatGPT can also remember things between chats. Memory is what the assistant picks up by itself, and the brief is what you wrote. Keep the brief, and once a month read what the assistant has saved about you. Both let you review memory and turn it off.

What to keep out

Client information

Never put passwords, card numbers or account numbers in the brief or in a chat. Say where they live and who may open them.

Before any client information goes into an assistant, check two things:

  • Your plan's data terms. OpenAI, for one, says it may use chats from personal ChatGPT accounts to improve its models unless you turn that setting off, and that it does not train on Business, Enterprise or Edu workspaces by default. Read the terms for the plan you pay for.
  • Your profession's rules. Lawyers have confidentiality duties under their state bar's rules, and ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) covers AI tools. Health practices have HIPAA. Staffing and real estate firms hold résumés, pay details and financial records that clients expect them to protect.

If you are unsure, write the brief with made-up examples and leave real records in the systems where they already live.

Part 3

Set what it may do alone

Two short lists, written into the brief.

May do alone

  • Fix something already agreed that broke
  • Re-send a confirmation that failed
  • Draft replies for a person to approve
  • Sort, tag and summarize incoming messages
  • Update its own notes
  • Flag a problem to the owner

Must ask first

  • Send anything to a new person for the first time
  • Spend money, or pass the monthly cost you set
  • Change prices, offer discounts, promise dates
  • Post anything in public under your name
  • Delete client data, or change who can log in
  • Anything new that nobody has discussed

The repair test

One test sorts most cases. A repair restores something you already approved: the confirmation text that failed, the form that stopped sending, the follow-up that went out late. Repairs need no permission. A new decision creates something you have not approved: a first message to a stranger, a discount, a public post. New decisions wait for a person.

Logins and access

Some assistants can now work in the background and connect to your email, calendar and other accounts. Give each one the narrowest access that does the job. Use a separate account where you can. Write down how to turn its access off, then test that you can.

Part 4

Check it every Monday

Fifteen minutes, done by the owner you named in Part 1.

A setup can work on day one and stop in week four. A form breaks, a login expires, a scheduled task pauses, or the person who read the summary stops reading it. Nothing announces the failure, so someone has to look.

1

Look at the number (3 minutes). Open the number from Part 1. Write this week's figure next to last week's, in the same place every week.

2

Test it yourself (5 minutes). Send one fake lead through the real path and confirm it reached the person or system that should act on it.

3

Fix one thing (5 minutes). If the number slipped, change one thing: the wording, the timing, or who gets the alert.

4

Write one line (2 minutes). What broke, what you changed, what you expect next week.

The fake-lead test

Once a month, and after any change to your website or phones, test every way a client can reach you.

  1. Use your own mobile phone and a personal email address, so you see what a stranger sees.
  2. Put the word TEST in the name field, so staff know to ignore it.
  3. Submit every form on the website, once on a phone and once on a computer.
  4. Call the main number during hours and after hours. Send a message to any text or chat line. Open any booking link.
  5. Email the address shown on your site and on your map listing.
  6. For each one, note when the message reached someone who will act on it. A "Thank you" page proves nothing until the message arrives.
  7. Delete the test records when you finish.

Stop rules

Decide in advance when the setup gets paused. Write the rules down with the name of the person who can pause it, and how.

  • A wrong or rude message reaches a client
  • Someone asks not to be contacted, and the setup contacts them again
  • The number slips two weeks in a row and one fix didn't help
  • Nobody did the Monday check for two weeks
  • Monthly cost passes the limit you set

Scheduled tasks can stop without telling you. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok can run jobs on a schedule. OpenAI's help page says a ChatGPT task may pause if it is inactive or needs your approval. The Monday check is how you find out.

Part 5

Your first 30 days

Print this page and tick it off.

Week 1: pick and measure

  • Name the leak, the number and the owner (Part 1)
  • Count the number for one week, before any AI
  • Write the one-page brief (Part 2)

Week 2: set limits and build small

  • Write the two lists and put them in the brief (Part 3)
  • See what the assistant and software you already pay for can do (Part 7, Appendix A)
  • Build the smallest version that handles the common case
  • Send a fake lead through it and confirm it arrived

Week 3: go live on the common case

  • Turn it on for the common case only. Odd cases go to a person.
  • Watch the first few runs yourself
  • Run the fake-lead test on every form and phone path
  • Write the stop rules and who can pause it

Week 4: first Monday checks

  • Do the 15-minute check (Part 4) and write one line
  • Fix one thing, or pause it
  • Update the brief with what you learned

Days 31 to 90

  • Every Monday: number, fake lead, one fix, one line
  • Day 45: confirm someone still reads the output and the number beats your starting count
  • Day 90: keep it, change one thing and run 90 more days, or stop it. Write down why.
  • Pick a second leak only after you decide to keep the first
Part 6 · Worked example

A small law office, filled in

An illustration we wrote to show the method. It is not a client and it reports no results.

The office has two lawyers and an office manager. New inquiries come in by web form and email. Those that arrive after 5 p.m. wait until someone reads the inbox the next day.

Part 1 · The leak

The job: acknowledge every new inquiry that arrives after hours, the same evening.

The number: after-hours inquiries each week, and how many were acknowledged before 9 a.m. the next business day.

Where it lives: the intake inbox. Nobody counts it yet.

Today's number: the office manager tallies it for one week.

Owner: the office manager, by name.

Part 2 · The brief, in short

Who we serve: people in our county with family-law matters.

What good looks like: every inquiry is acknowledged the same evening, and a person calls the next business day.

Decisions already made: we give no legal advice before a signed engagement. We quote no fees in writing before a consultation. We do not take criminal matters.

Where the numbers live: the intake inbox and the practice-management calendar. The office manager and both lawyers can see them.

How we sound: calm, short, respectful. First names.

Part 3 · The two lists

May do alone: send the fixed acknowledgment below. Sort inquiries by type. Build the morning call list. Text the office manager if a message mentions a court date or a deadline.

Must ask first: any reply about the facts of a matter. Any mention of fees. Whether the office will take the matter. Any message to another party. Conflict checks stay with a lawyer.

The message it sends

"Thank you for contacting [Firm]. We received your message. [Name] will call you on [next business day] between [hours]. Please do not send confidential details by email until we have spoken. This message is not legal advice and does not create an attorney-client relationship."

A staffing firm can use the same shape for new applicants, and a real estate office for new listing inquiries. Change the job, the number and the two lists. A blank copy is at the end of this guide.

Part 7

Three first jobs, and how to get them built

Each is a starting shape. Your number tells you whether it works.

Start with the software you already pay for. Ask its support line the question shown. If the feature exists, turn it on and run the fake-lead test. If it does not, that build is the part you hire for, and Parts 1 to 3 are what you hand the builder.

Job 1 · Missed-call text-back

What it does: when a call goes unanswered, it sends one short text. Replies go to a person.

Ask your phone provider: "Do you offer missed-call text-back or an automatic reply text?"

Text to use: "Hi, this is [Business]. Sorry we missed your call. Reply here or book a time at [link]."

Must ask first: anything after the first text, and any quote or price.

Number: missed calls each week, and how many got a text within the window you set.

Job 2 · Quote follow-up at 48 hours

What it does: when a quote has had no reply for 48 hours, it drafts a short follow-up in your voice. For the first month a person approves each one.

Ask your quoting or customer software provider: "Can you flag quotes with no reply after 48 hours and draft a follow-up?"

Stops when: the client replies, accepts, declines or asks you to stop. One follow-up unless you set more.

Number: quotes sent, follow-ups sent on time, and replies received.

Job 3 · Appointment confirmation

What it does: it confirms each booking before the visit and gives an easy way to reschedule.

Ask your scheduling software provider: "Do you send reminders with confirm and reschedule links?"

Stops when: the client confirms, cancels, reschedules or replies STOP. It never sends at night, and never to someone who has not agreed to texts. Check the texting consent rules that apply to you with your phone provider or lawyer.

Number: bookings, confirmations and no-shows each week.

Part 8

If you hire someone

Eight questions to ask any builder, including us.

  1. What is the first job, and what number will show it works?
  2. Who owns the weekly check after you leave, and what do they look at?
  3. What do you add beyond the projects, memory and scheduled tasks in the assistant we already pay for?
  4. Which of our accounts will it hold, and how do we turn its access off?
  5. When it fails, who finds out, and how fast?
  6. What does it cost each month to run, and is there a cap?
  7. Who owns the setup and the brief if we stop working together?
  8. What would make you tell us not to automate this?

A careful builder will also question you. Expect to be asked what happens between a new lead and a paid invoice, what you have tried before, and who on your side will own the job.

Two mistakes to avoid

Going live without a test. Nothing is finished until a test message has arrived at the real destination. Test again after every change.

Switching tools because a new one launched. Each switch means new setup, new logins and a brief to rewrite. Change tools when a specific job keeps failing on the current one. Give a new tool 30 days against your number before you commit.

What four assistants do today

This is not a ranking. Each line comes from the vendor page linked beside it, read on the date above. We left out products we could not confirm on the vendor's own page. Before you pay anyone to build, see what your current plan already does.

Claude (Anthropic). Projects hold their own instructions and files. Memory across chats can be paused, reset or turned off. Scheduled tasks run on paid plans, and Claude shows when a run failed or needs your input.

Projects · Memory · Scheduled tasks · Changelog

ChatGPT (OpenAI). Scheduled tasks run once or on repeat, and are listed on a page called Scheduled. Free accounts can run a repeating task at most once a day. Paid plans can run one as often as hourly. Plus, Pro and Business accounts can also start a task from a new Gmail message, Slack message or GitHub pull request. A task may pause if it is inactive or needs your approval. Memory can be reviewed, corrected and turned off under Settings, Personalization, Memory.

Scheduled tasks · Memory and data use · Projects

Gemini (Google). Scheduled actions run a prompt at a set time or on repeat, on Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra plans. Skills are saved instructions you reuse. They replace Gems, which Google makes read-only on November 17, 2026 and removes on December 17, 2026 for personal accounts.

Plans and limits · Gems to skills

Grok (xAI). Automations run a job you describe on a schedule and report by email or app notice. Starting a job from an incoming email needs a SuperGrok plan.
