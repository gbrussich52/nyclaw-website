Start here

This guide is for an owner or office manager who wants an AI assistant to take one repeating job off the firm's plate. You need no technical background. The method takes about ten minutes to read.

The method has seven steps. Name one leak that costs you money. Write a one-page brief. Show the assistant what good work looks like. Decide what it may do alone. Make it check its own work. Write down anything you had to explain twice. Then check the result yourself, every week.

In this guide, the operator is the person who owns that one job: who sets it up, checks it each week, and can turn it off.

After the seven steps come pages you use: a 30-day checklist, a filled-in example, words to copy and paste, and a one-page plan. The notes at the back give the detail behind each step, and every source is numbered and listed under References.

1

Name one leak

One job, one number, one owner. Everything else waits.

Start with a leak you can count. Some common ones:

  • Trades and front desks: missed calls with no reply, quotes with no follow-up, appointments nobody confirms.
  • Law offices: new inquiries that arrive after hours and wait.
  • Staffing and recruiting: candidates who apply and hear nothing, and clients who wait on a status update.
  • Real estate: new listing inquiries that go unanswered, and showings nobody confirms.

Pick the one that costs you most.

Write five things down

The jobFor example: reply to every missed call with a text
The number that shows it is doneFor example: missed calls this week, and how many got a text
Where that number lives todayA report, a spreadsheet, or "nowhere yet"
Today's number, before any AICount it for one week
Who owns itOne person's name

If the number lives nowhere, your first job is to count. Without a starting number you cannot tell later whether the setup helped.

Keep the job small. Automate the common case and send the odd cases to a person.

Notes on step 1

2

Write the one-page brief

An assistant that doesn't know how your firm works will guess.

Anthropic's guide to writing instructions for Claude starts from one picture:

Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows. Anthropic, "Prompting best practices"1

So write the brief the way you would brief a sharp new hire on day one.

Who we serveThe clients you want and what they usually need
What good looks likeFor example: every inquiry gets a reply within the hour during business hours
Decisions already made, and whyPrices you don't discount, work you don't take, words you never use
Where the facts liveWhich system holds leads, quotes, bookings and invoices, and who can see each
How we soundFor example: warm, short, no slang. Samples come in Part 3.
What it may do alone, and what it must ask aboutFrom Part 4

Give the reason with the rule

A bare rule covers only the case you thought of. A rule with its reason covers the cases next to it. Compare:

  • Rule only: "Never quote a fee by email."
  • Rule with reason: "Don't quote a fee by email, because fees depend on facts we only learn in the consultation. Offer a call instead."

The second version also tells the assistant what to do.

Tibo, who works on Codex and ChatGPT at OpenAI, said this of one user's feature request in July 2026: "The combination of {motivation, explanation, screenshot} forms a perfect prompt for Codex." That is the brief in small: why you want it, what you want, and a picture of it.2

What to keep out

Client information

Never put passwords, card numbers or account numbers in the brief or in a chat. Say where they live and who may open them. Before any client information goes into an assistant, check your plan's data terms and your profession's rules. If you are unsure, write the brief with made-up examples and leave real records in the systems where they already live.

Notes on step 2

3

Show it what good looks like

Three real examples teach more than a page of adjectives.

"Warm and professional" means something different to every reader, and to every assistant. A message you actually sent means one thing.

Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure. Anthropic, "Prompting best practices"1

Build a small reference pack once.

Three to five pieces you were proud of

Pull real messages your firm sent: a reply to a new inquiry, a quote follow-up, a note that calmed an upset client. Take out names and private details first.

Choose pieces that differ. Include one hard case, such as a reply to someone you had to turn away.

Your voice, described from your own writing

You don't have to describe your own voice. Ask the assistant to do it from your own writing, then correct what it gets wrong.

Read my sent messages and tell me about my tone and writing style. Anthropic, Claude Academy, "Write in my voice"3

Save the description it gives you into the brief.

4

Set what it may do alone

Two short lists, written into the brief.

May do alone

  • Fix something already agreed that broke
  • Re-send a confirmation that failed
  • Draft replies for a person to approve
  • Sort, tag and summarize incoming messages
  • Update its own notes
  • Flag a problem to the owner

Must ask first

  • Send anything to a new person for the first time
  • Spend money, or pass the monthly cost you set
  • Change prices, offer discounts, promise dates
  • Post anything in public under your name
  • Delete client data, or change who can log in
  • Anything new that nobody has discussed

When something fits both lists, it goes on the right.

Start with drafts only

For the first month, the assistant writes and a person presses send. Anthropic describes its small-business package this way:

Claude does the work; you approve before anything sends, posts, or pays. Anthropic, "Introducing Claude for Small Business"4

Approve before the send, because nothing afterward takes it back. A weekly check will find Friday's wrong email. It will not unsend it.

Logins, access, and messages that give orders

Some assistants can now work in the background and connect to your email, calendar and files. Give each one the narrowest access that does the job. Use a separate account where you can. Write down how to turn the access off, then test that you can.

An assistant that reads your inbox reads whatever strangers send you, and a message can be written to give it orders. An assistant that cannot send, pay or delete without your yes is much harder to turn against you. Still read what you approve, because a planted message can shape the draft in front of you.

Notes on step 4

5

Make it check its own work

A stated standard, proof, and a second look.

An assistant will tell you a job is finished when it is not. Give the assistant a way to check, and ask to see the check.

Boris Cherny, who created Claude Code at Anthropic, calls this "probably the most important thing" for getting good results from it: "give Claude a way to verify its work. If Claude has that feedback loop, it will 2-3x the quality of the final result."5 For a programmer the check is a test that runs. Yours is the pass-or-fail list in the next section.

1. Give it a standard it can pass or fail

"Make it good" cannot be checked. A short list can. Anthropic suggests the wording:

Before you finish, verify your answer against [test criteria]. Anthropic, "Prompting best practices"1

For a reply to a new inquiry, the criteria might be:

  • Under 120 words
  • Uses the client's name and the firm's name
  • States no price, no date we haven't confirmed, and no advice
  • Says who will call and when
  • Every fact comes from the inquiry or the brief

Ask it to list any rule the draft fails. A draft that comes back with "fails rule 3: mentions a fee" has saved you a mistake.

2. Ask for proof, and for where each fact came from

  • "Show me." The sent message, the calendar entry, the row in the spreadsheet.
  • "Where did that come from?" For any date, price, name or deadline, the assistant names the document or system it read. If it cannot, the fact does not go out.

3. Get a second look from a reader that didn't write it

A writer rereads its own draft kindly. A fresh reader does not. In an office, that means opening a new chat with no history, pasting in the draft and the checklist, and asking what is wrong with it. Use it for anything that goes to many people at once or that would be costly to get wrong.

What we have found: a second reader catches what the writer misses. One checked this guide against its sources before it went out and found a date that was off by a day.

4. A person still signs

None of this replaces you. Anthropic's Claude Code team wrote about how they review work their assistant produces, and two of their rules fit any firm:

An agent's approval never counts as a person's, either. Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt and Qing Wang, "How the Claude Code team does code review"6

The other is that whoever asked for the work answers for it.6 In your firm that is the operator from Part 1.

Read every word of anything that carries money, a deadline or your name.

Notes on step 5

6

Write it down once

If you explained it twice, it belongs in writing.

You will correct the assistant in the first weeks. That is normal. The mistake is correcting the same thing a third time. Anthropic's test for what belongs in an assistant's standing instructions includes one plain item:

Anything you've had to correct twice already Anthropic, "Best practices for Claude Code"7

Boris Cherny passed on the same habit from the Claude Code team in one line. After every correction, end with: "Update your CLAUDE.md so you don't make that mistake again." CLAUDE.md is the file of standing rules that Claude Code loads into every session. He adds: "Claude is eerily good at writing rules for itself." Try it with your own assistant. After you correct it, ask it to write the rule, then read the rule before you keep it.8

Lauren Tan, who works on Grok Bot at SpaceXSI, goes one step further. The point of one of her own posts, she wrote, is "to move stuff out of prose and make it deterministic." Prose is the written instructions. Deterministic means it happens the same way every time, whatever the assistant decides. In an office that is a required field on the intake form or a fixed template, not a tenth sentence in the brief.9

  • Standing rules say how the firm works, on every job. They live in the brief.
  • A saved method says how to do one repeating job, step by step. Claude, Gemini and Grok Bot all call this a skill.

Keep this week's special case out of both. Put it in the message where you ask for the work.

By hand first, then saved, then scheduled

The order matters. SpaceXSI's documentation gives it in one sentence:

Make it reliable, save the method as a skill, and only then automate it. SpaceXSI, Grok Bot documentation, "Skills and routines"10

Run the job by hand, with you reading every result, until it comes out right several times in a row. Then save the method. Put it on a schedule last. A job that is scheduled before it is reliable makes the same mistake every morning.

Keep it short, and prune it

Every line of standing instructions is read on every job. Read the whole file once a quarter. Cut any rule whose reason has gone.

Notes on step 6

7

Check it every Monday

Fifteen minutes, done by the owner you named in Part 1.

Check it every Monday, or whichever day suits your week. Put it on the calendar with a name next to it. A check that isn't scheduled doesn't happen, and the assistant won't check itself.

A setup can work on day one and stop in week four. A form breaks, a login expires, a scheduled job pauses, or the person who read the summary stops reading it. Nothing announces the failure.

The fifteen minutes

  1. Look at the number (3 minutes). Open the number from Part 1. Write this week's figure next to last week's, in the same place every week.
  2. Read the record (3 minutes). Open the assistant's list of recent runs. Look for any that failed, did not run, or are waiting on you. Read two or three of the things it actually sent.
  3. Test it yourself (5 minutes). Send one fake lead through the real path and confirm it reached the person or system that should act on it.
  4. Fix one thing (2 minutes). If something slipped, change one thing: the wording, the timing, or who gets the alert.
  5. Write one line (2 minutes). What broke, what you changed, what you expect next week.

The fake-lead test

Once a month, and after any change to your website or phones, test every way a client can reach you.

  1. Use your own mobile phone and a personal email address.
  2. Put the word TEST in the name field, so staff know to ignore it.
  3. Submit every form on the website, once on a phone and once on a computer.
  4. Call the main number during hours and after hours. Send a message to any text or chat line. Open any booking link.
  5. Email the address shown on your site and on your map listing.
  6. For each one, note when the message reached someone who will act on it, and whether the visitor got what the page promised. A "Thank you" page proves nothing until the message arrives.
  7. Delete the test records when you finish.

What we have come across: a form on our own site that showed a thank-you page and sent no email. That is why the test asks whether the visitor got what the page promised.

Stop rules

Decide in advance when the setup gets paused.

  • A wrong or rude message reaches a client
  • Someone asks not to be contacted, and the setup contacts them again
  • The number slips two weeks in a row and one fix didn't help
  • Nobody did the weekly check for two weeks
  • Monthly cost passes the limit you set

Your first 30 days

Print it and tick it off.

Week 1: pick, measure, brief

  • Name the leak, the number and the owner (Part 1)
  • Count the number for one week, before any AI
  • Write the one-page brief and run the colleague test (Part 2)
  • Gather three to five pieces you were proud of (Part 3)

Week 2: set limits and work by hand

  • Write the two lists and put them in the brief (Part 4)
  • See what the assistant and software you already pay for can do (Part 10, Appendix A)
  • Run the job by hand, drafts only, and read every one
  • Write the checklist the drafts must pass (Part 5)

Week 3: save the method, go live on the common case

  • Save the method once it comes out right several times running (Part 6)
  • Turn it on for the common case only. Odd cases go to a person.
  • Run the fake-lead test on every form and phone path
  • Write the stop rules and who can pause it
  • Put the weekly check on the calendar, with a name

Week 4: first weekly checks

  • Do the 15-minute check (Part 7) and write one line
  • Fix one thing, or pause it
  • Add anything you corrected twice to the instructions

Days 31 to 90

  • Every week: number, record, fake lead, one fix, one line
  • Day 45: confirm someone still reads the output and the number beats your starting count
  • Day 90: keep it, change one thing and run 90 more days, or stop it. Write down why.
  • Pick a second leak only after you decide to keep the first

A small law office, filled in

An illustration we wrote to show the method. It is not a client and it reports no results.

The office has two lawyers and an office manager. New inquiries come in by web form and email. Those that arrive after 5 p.m. wait until someone reads the inbox the next day.

Part 1: the leak

The jobAcknowledge every new inquiry that arrives after hours, the same evening.
The numberAfter-hours inquiries each week, and how many were acknowledged before 9 a.m. the next business day.
Where it livesThe intake inbox. Nobody counts it yet, so the office manager tallies it for one week.
OwnerThe office manager, by name.

Part 2: the brief, in short

Who we servePeople in our county with family-law matters.
What good looks likeEvery inquiry is acknowledged the same evening, and a person calls the next business day.
Decisions already made, and whyWe give no legal advice before a signed engagement, because advice creates duties we have not agreed to take on. We quote no fees in writing before a consultation, because fees depend on facts we learn there. We do not take criminal matters.
Where the facts liveThe intake inbox and the practice-management calendar. The office manager and both lawyers can see them.

Part 3: what good looks like

ExamplesFour past replies with names removed: a routine inquiry, a frightened caller, a matter we declined, a request we referred elsewhere.
VoiceCalm, short, respectful. First names. No legal terms the reader has to look up.
Dislike, and what to do insteadThe old auto-reply ("Your message has been received and will be reviewed"). Instead: say who will call, and when.
TemplateThe acknowledgment below, word for word.

Part 4: the two lists

May do aloneSend the fixed acknowledgment. Sort inquiries by type. Build the morning call list. Text the office manager if a message mentions a court date or a deadline.
Must ask firstAny reply about the facts of a matter. Any mention of fees. Whether the office will take the matter. Any message to another party. Conflict checks stay with a lawyer.

Part 5: the check it runs before anything goes out

The message matches the template word for word, apart from the name and the day. It went to the address on the inquiry and nowhere else. The call list names every inquiry received since 5 p.m., and each entry says where it came from.

The message it sends

"Thank you for contacting [Firm]. We received your message. [Name] will call you on [next business day] between [hours]. Please do not send confidential details by email until we have spoken. This message is not legal advice and does not create an attorney-client relationship."

Part 6. After two weeks of reading every acknowledgment, the office manager saves the method under the name "After-hours inquiry." Part 7. The weekly check goes on the office manager's calendar for Monday at 9:15, and the first step is last week's tally.

A staffing firm can use the same shape for new applicants, and a real estate office for new listing inquiries. Change the job, the number, the examples and the two lists. A blank copy is in Appendix C.

Three first jobs, and how to get them built

Each is a starting shape. Your number tells you whether it works.

Start with the software you already pay for. Ask its support line the question shown. If the feature exists, turn it on and run the fake-lead test. If it does not, that build is the part you hire for, and Parts 1 to 6 are what you hand the builder.

Job 1: missed-call text-back

What it doesWhen a call goes unanswered, it sends one short text. Replies go to a person.
Ask your phone provider"Do you offer missed-call text-back or an automatic reply text?"
Text to use"Hi, this is [Business]. Sorry we missed your call. Reply here or book a time at [link]."
Must ask firstAnything after the first text, and any quote or price.
NumberMissed calls each week, and how many got a text within the window you set.

Job 2: quote follow-up at 48 hours

What it doesWhen a quote has had no reply for 48 hours, it drafts a short follow-up in your voice. For the first month a person approves each one.
Ask your quoting or customer software provider"Can you flag quotes with no reply after 48 hours and draft a follow-up?"
Stops whenThe client replies, accepts, declines or asks you to stop. One follow-up unless you set more.
NumberQuotes sent, follow-ups sent on time, and replies received.

Job 3: appointment confirmation

What it doesIt confirms each booking before the visit and gives an easy way to reschedule.
Ask your scheduling software provider"Do you send reminders with confirm and reschedule links?"
Stops whenThe client confirms, cancels, reschedules or replies STOP. It never sends at night, and never to someone who has not agreed to texts. Check the texting consent rules that apply to you with your phone provider or lawyer.
NumberBookings, confirmations and no-shows each week.
11

If you hire someone

Eight questions to ask any builder, including us.

  1. What is the first job, and what number will show it works?
  2. Who owns the weekly check after you leave, and what do they look at?
  3. What do you add beyond the projects, skills and scheduled tasks in the assistant we already pay for?
  4. Which of our accounts will it hold, and how do we turn its access off?
  5. When it fails, who finds out, and how fast?
  6. What does it cost each month to run, and is there a cap?
  7. Who owns the setup, the brief and the saved methods if we stop working together?
  8. What would make you tell us not to automate this?

A careful builder will also question you. Expect to be asked what happens between a new lead and a paid invoice, what you have tried before, and who on your side will own the job.

Two mistakes to avoid

Going live without a test. Nothing is finished until a test message has arrived at the real destination. Test again after every change.

Switching tools because a new one launched. Each switch means new setup, new logins and a brief to rewrite. Change tools when a specific job keeps failing on the current one. Give a new tool 30 days against your number before you commit.

Notes on each step

More detail on each step, for when you want it. Read the step first.

Step 1: Name one leak

Keep the job small. Anthropic gives builders the same advice:

When building applications with LLMs, we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed. Anthropic, "Building effective agents"25

Back to step 1

Step 2: Write the one-page brief

The colleague test

Before you use the brief, hand it to someone in the office who was not involved. Anthropic's test is short:

Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they'd be confused, Claude will be too. Anthropic, "Prompting best practices"1

Where to put it

Most assistants have a place for standing instructions, so you don't paste the brief into every chat. In Claude and ChatGPT it is a project. In Gemini it is a skill. In Grok Bot you tell the assistant a lasting rule in plain words. Appendix A lists the vendor pages.

Several assistants also remember things between chats. Memory is what the assistant picks up by itself, and the brief is what you wrote. Keep the brief, and once a month read what the assistant has saved about you. For anything that matters, have it look up the fact again. SpaceXSI, the company that makes Grok, says it plainly in its documentation:

Memory is not a substitute for an authoritative source SpaceXSI, Grok Bot documentation, "Create and manage Bots"26

What to keep out

Before any client information goes into an assistant, check two things:

  • Your plan's data terms. OpenAI, for one, says it may use chats from personal ChatGPT accounts to improve its models unless you turn that setting off, and that it does not train on Business, Enterprise or Edu workspaces by default. Read the terms for the plan you pay for.17
  • Your profession's rules. Lawyers have confidentiality duties under their state bar's rules, and ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024) covers a lawyer's use of generative AI.27 Health practices have HIPAA. Staffing and real estate firms hold résumés, pay details and financial records that clients expect them to protect.

Back to step 2

Step 3: Show it what good looks like

Pieces you like, and pieces you don't

Keep a short running list in two columns. On one side, things you would be glad to have sent. On the other, things that made you wince: the stiff form letter, the reply that buried the answer in the third paragraph.

For every piece you dislike, write one line on what to do instead. "Too long" tells the assistant little. "Too long. Give the answer in the first sentence and stop at five" gives it something to do.

This list is our own practice. The vendors recommend good examples and clear instructions; none we read tells you to collect bad examples, and Anthropic's advice is to say what you want rather than only what you don't.1

A one-page brand sheet

If the assistant makes anything a client will see, give it the firm's look and wording in one place.

The firm's name, exactly as writtenAnd how you sign off
Words we use, and words we never useFor example: "clients," never "customers."
Logo, colors and typefacesThe files, plus a screenshot of your letterhead or website
Two or three websites or documents we admireOne line each on what you like

Add a template where the shape repeats

Where a message has the same parts every time, hand over the skeleton: greeting, what we received, what happens next, when, sign-off. Tell the assistant whether the template is exact or a starting point.

Back to step 3

Step 4: Set what it may do alone

The two lists also guard against the opposite mistake: an assistant that asks about everything. Tibo at OpenAI describes a setting "that requires you to approve everything" as "prone to decision fatigue unless you spend a lot of time configuring specific rules." A tired approver stops reading. The lists are those rules.28

Anthropic's safety page for its desktop assistant suggests "a dedicated working folder" in place of broad access, and backups of anything important.15 The same page warns about messages written to give an assistant orders, which Anthropic calls prompt injection.15

The repair test

A repair restores something you already approved: the confirmation text that failed, the form that stopped sending. Repairs need no permission. A new decision creates something you have not approved: a first message to a stranger, a discount, a public post. New decisions wait for a person.

The repair test is our own practice.

Back to step 4

Step 5: Make it check its own work

A second look has limits. Lauren Tan, who works on Grok Bot at SpaceXSI, wrote in November 2026 that she now considers "adversarial reviews mostly a waste of tokens" and added that she meant "just having agents review code without running it" or checking it with a tool. An adversarial review is one AI looking for faults in another's work. Her reason: the reviewers "likely have 0 context around your goals and intent, and they tend to pick up things that don't matter" or push the work the wrong way. She was writing about code, and the point carries. Keep the order of step 5: the pass-or-fail list first, proof second, the fresh reader third. The checklist you paste in is what tells the fresh reader your goals.293031

Asked why anyone still needs to read the code, Boris Cherny answered: "I read diffs when it's touching critical code, or code that's important to get right." A diff is the list of changes the assistant made.32

One more caution, from an Anthropic study published October 27, 2026. Fifty professional analysts each worked with an AI agent that had been set to lean one way on each choice. They went with the agent's lean 86% of the time, where chance would give about 50%. The ones who followed it most closely were about as likely as the rest to call the work their own judgment. The authors strengthened the lean on purpose, so everyday assistants may sway people less. For decisions that are yours, such as whether to take a matter or what to charge, ask for the strongest case for each option before you ask what it would do.33

The Claude Code team's rule for when to save a method: "If you do something more than once a day, turn it into a skill or command" (Boris Cherny, January 2026).34

Two people who build these tools give reasons for keeping instructions short, from their own work. Lauren Tan, on her own set of saved methods: "the more capable models get, the less instructions they need."35 Tibo at OpenAI, listing fixes his team had shipped in September 2026: "Some skills written for previous models were triggering too often or preventing the model from checking its work."36 An instruction that helped last year's assistant can hold back this year's.

Step 7: Check it every Monday

You can ask the assistant to bring its own list to the check. Ask yours for three lines: what it finished, where it was unsure, what is waiting on you. Read the list, then do your own check anyway.

SpaceXSI's documentation says Grok Bot may ask whether to keep scheduled routines running after you have been away, and "pause them if there is no response."10 OpenAI's help page says a ChatGPT task may pause if it is inactive or needs your approval.16 A job that waits on an approval nobody sees has stopped, whatever the screen says.
